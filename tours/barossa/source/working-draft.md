## Script: Stop 1 — The Dolphin That Started a Fashion

*About 390 words, about 3 minutes.*

[SFX — harbour, under]

Every species has its fashions. This one began over Christmas, with a dolphin who couldn't find her way out.

On Boxing Day, nineteen eighty-seven, a young bottlenose dolphin from this estuary became trapped in a harbour. After about two weeks she was caught and taken to a nearby dolphinarium to recover. The scientists who later told her story call her Billie.

Her new neighbours were five trained performers, and one of their show tricks was the tail-walk: a dolphin rears up out of the water and holds itself there, upright, by beating its tail. Nobody trained Billie. She just watched. In late January nineteen eighty-eight she was released near where she'd been caught, back into the wild.

[SFX — harbour, stop]

Seven years later, in nineteen ninety-five, observers saw Billie tail-walk in the wild.

[SFX — dolphin-blow]

Another female, Wave, took it up with real enthusiasm. From two thousand and seven to two thousand and fourteen, Wave alone made most of the tail-walks recorded each year. Eleven dolphins in this community were seen doing it, adult females and youngsters, and never once an adult male. Calves whose mothers had never tail-walked learned it anyway. And it was useless: it burns energy and catches no fish. But three times in four, other dolphins were watching.

Then it went out of style. Tail-walking peaked around twenty ten and twenty eleven, and faded. Billie had died in two thousand and nine. Wave vanished in two thousand and fourteen, but the fade had already begun, and the researchers don't claim to know why.

After more than thirty thousand hours of watching, they call social learning highly likely, while saying plainly that this was observation, not an experiment. They file it with other animal fads: Japanese macaques who took up handling stones, and orcas in Puget Sound who for a while carried dead salmon about. And they make a sharper point. A few weeks in a dolphinarium was still showing up in the wild two decades later.

All of this happened in the Adelaide Dolphin Sanctuary, inside South Australia's largest port: working ships above, and below, a mangrove forest ten thousand years old, seagrass, tidal creeks, and the wrecks of at least forty abandoned vessels.

So, one for later: a habit that spreads by copying, does nothing useful, and fades when the trendsetters go. Is that culture, or just a fad? And which of your own habits would pass the test?

## Script: Stop 2 — The Map That Leaves Things Out

*About 470 words, about 3.6 minutes, with the song segment.*

[SFX — plains, under]

Any map on a phone makes this stretch look simple: the Adelaide Plains, a few highways, the Barossa ahead. It's an excellent map for a bus. It leaves out nearly everything.

These plains are Kaurna Country. Kaurna people are the Traditional Custodians of the Adelaide Plains, of Kaurna Yerta, the land, and Kauwi, the waters. And that isn't the past tense. The Kaurna Yerta Aboriginal Corporation describes a native title boundary running from Lower Light, to the north, to Myponga in the south, and a culture that Kaurna people have continued to practise, evolve and share.

Where we're heading, the Barossa acknowledges three peoples: Ngadjuri, Peramangk and Kaurna. Their Country doesn't follow the lines of modern roads, and this story won't pretend to draw it.

[SFX — plains, stop]

Now a second map, a paper one.

[SFX — parchment]

On the nineteenth of February, eighteen thirty-six, King William the Fourth signed the Letters Patent that created the Province of South Australia and fixed its borders. It was to be a free settlement, not a convict colony. And it came with a promise. Written into the document is a proviso that nothing in it should affect the rights of the Aboriginal Natives of the Province to the actual occupation or enjoyment of their lands.

That sentence was a compromise, and the interesting part is who was arguing. The Colonial Office in London believed Aboriginal people held real property rights in their land. The colony's planners argued that Aboriginal people didn't occupy land in any way British institutions would recognise. The words that survived the argument were a promise. Colonisation, in the measured phrase of South Australia's own Centre of Democracy, proceeded with little regard for them.

The original is held in the State Records of South Australia, and the Centre calls it, still, a source of discussion and controversy.

So two maps of the same ground. One of highways and cellar doors. One of Country, language and responsibility that never stopped. Today's tour runs on the first. The second is underneath all of it.

Here's one for the next glass: a promise written into a founding document, then ignored. Does it still bind anyone, nearly two centuries on? Or is it only history?

Another living language is in today's music. Not Kaurna this time, but Nukunu, from the Southern Flinders Ranges further north. Tilly Tjala Thomas grew up in Adelaide, and her father is a Nukunu man. On drives through the Flinders, he would ask her a question in Nukunu, ngana nyunyi, which means "what's that?", and she would answer. That car game became a song, which she wrote partly with her dad, in Nukunu and in English. It's called Ngana Nyunyi, and it was made for a long drive. If you'd like to hear it, tap the song button on the screen.

## Script: Stop 3 — The Bottles That Popped Under the Huts

*About 455 words, about 3.5 minutes.*

In September nineteen forty-one, just after Britain and the Soviet Union invaded Iran, British forces there arrested a German-speaking civilian named Hermann Thumm.

He'd been born in nineteen twelve, to German Lutheran parents, in a village in Georgia called Katharinenfeld, today Bolnisi, into a family that made wine. He was no soldier. He was a civilian, and in November nineteen forty-one he landed in Adelaide as an internee, bound for a camp at Loveday, and later another at Tatura. He wouldn't be released until August nineteen forty-six.

Half a century on, he wrote about those years in a memoir, The Road to Yaldara. What follows are his own words from that book, in a voice standing in for his.

[SFX — camp-night, under]

So, Mr Thumm. Nearly five years behind the wire. How did you spend them?

THUMM: It was a time for pondering and learning, for whoever wanted to work could work but the wise ones used the time to study.

And you were one of the wise ones?

THUMM: I really and honestly can say that three out of those years I spent in the library studying hard.

Studying for what? A way home?

THUMM: I was not a great believer in post-war Europe and prepared myself for a life in Australia.

And the wine? Surely that wasn't allowed.

THUMM: Winemaking was unofficially tolerated if we behaved ourselves.

And did it behave itself?

THUMM: In the camp my champagne in beer bottles was popping all over and under the huts.

[SFX — bottle-pop]

[SFX — camp-night, stop]

The chemistry is simple. Fermentation turns grape sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide. Trap that gas in a sealed bottle and it has to go somewhere. Under the huts, it went somewhere.

Released in nineteen forty-six, he found work at a winery near Lyndoch. Driving past an old ruin with his boss one afternoon, the estate's own history says, his imagination caught. It had been a water-driven flour mill on Lyndoch Creek in the eighteen-fifties, then a flax works, then a winery that closed in nineteen sixteen. He talked the owners into selling, and in nineteen forty-seven he made his first vintage there, from local growers' grapes. When he married, the family's first home was rooms in the old mill.

And the grand château on this estate, the one that looks as if it has stood for centuries? It went up in the nineteen-sixties, built around the flax works' old retting tanks, and its first purpose was to hold his collections of furniture and fine china.

Here's one for the glass. He called those years a time for pondering and learning, and he built a life out of them. Can you admire what someone made of a place, without for a moment approving of their being put there?

## Script: Stop 4 — The Wine That Must Not Be Bottled

*About 590 words, about 4.5 minutes, with the song segment.*

[SFX — cellar, under]

In eighteen seventy-eight, Benno Seppelt finished a new cellar on this estate, chose a single cask of his finest wine, a puncheon, and gave an instruction: it was not to be bottled for a hundred years.

Think about who that instruction was for. Not him. Not anyone who tasted the wine that autumn. It could only be carried out by people who hadn't been born yet, and it trusted them, sight unseen, to keep their hands off.

The family had come a long way to be able to think like that. Benno's father, Joseph Seppelt, had run a tobacco, snuff and liqueur business in Silesia. He sailed from Hamburg in eighteen forty-nine, and two years later he and his wife Johanna founded Seppeltsfield, barely fifteen years after the colony itself began.

[SFX — cask-knock]

Benno died long before his deadline. He and his wife Sophie lie in the family mausoleum here, finished in nineteen twenty-seven. And in nineteen seventy-eight, exactly as instructed, the estate made its first release of a hundred-year-old tawny from that cellar. The promise was kept by people born long after the wine was made.

[SFX — cellar, stop]

Here's the part that turns a stunt into a system. The estate says it has kept going ever since, with a barrel from every vintage, back unbroken to eighteen seventy-eight, each one waiting out its hundred years. So the cellar is less a store than a timeline.

Benno thought in long lines in stone, too. In eighteen eighty-eight he finished a winery built into the hillside on terraces, so gravity could carry the fruit down through the stages of making wine. A newspaper of the day called it an object lesson to the whole colony. It was retired in the nineteen-eighties, sat as a museum for nearly thirty years, and was brought back to work for the twenty ten vintage. Old ideas, it turns out, can wait in a cellar too.

Now the outdoor version. On the roads into the estate, look out for the palms. Planting began in nineteen twenty-nine, just as the Great Depression arrived, and the family's own account counts more than two thousand trees in all, planted over years, not in a day. South Australia's History Trust records that the palm avenues, along with pine plantations, gave people work through the Depression. So the palms are a monument of a sort, to hard times, and to an estate that spent its money on something that would only look like this long after the planters had gone home.

A barrel no one could open in their lifetime. Avenues planted for a future nobody could see. The thread through all of it is a patience that feels almost alien now.

So, for the next glass: what would you start today that only someone else could finish? And who would you trust to follow the instructions?

There's only one song for a wine that waits a century. Jim Croce's Time In A Bottle began as an album track in nineteen seventy-two, never expected to be a single. Then, as the people who made the record tell it, it was used in an American television film about a week before Croce was killed in a plane crash in September nineteen seventy-three. Radio stations were flooded with callers asking who sang it. By December it was number one, about three months after his death: a song about saving time, a hit only after his had run out. If you'd like to hear it, tap the song button on the screen.

## Script: Stop 5 — The Towns That Changed Their Names

*About 455 words, about 3.5 minutes.*

[SFX — village, under]

By now you'll have noticed that this valley speaks with a German accent. The usual explanation is religious refugees, and it's about half true.

In eighteen seventeen, the King of Prussia ordered his subjects to use his new worship book and join his new Union Church. The Old Lutherans who refused could be fined, imprisoned, or have their belongings taken. Between eighteen thirty-eight and eighteen forty, nearly five hundred of them came from Brandenburg with their pastor, August Kavel, and in eighteen forty-one more than two hundred others arrived, most of whom founded Bethanien, now Bethany, here in the Barossa.

[SFX — village, stop]

Here's the other half. By eighteen fifty-one almost seven thousand Germans had come, and only about half as religious communities. The rest came for land and a better living, after failed harvests and a slump in weaving back home. Nor was the voyage charity. George Fife Angas, the founder of the South Australian Company, lent the Old Lutherans the money to come, then sold them land at ten times what he'd paid for it, at twenty per cent interest. And they weren't quite Germans: they came decades before there was a Germany to come from.

By nineteen eleven, Lutherans were almost one South Australian in fourteen. Then came the First World War, and promoting any part of German culture was forbidden. A German-language newspaper published since eighteen fifty was banned. Dozens of schools that taught in German were closed. And in January nineteen eighteen, under the Nomenclature Act, dozens of German place names were struck off the map, and using the old ones in an address or a legal document was forbidden.

[SFX — pen-scratch]

The strange part is what replaced them. The committee that drew up the list recommended Aboriginal names, citing a popular desire for the preservation of aboriginal patronymics. Parliament dropped most of them and reached for the war instead: Loos, Vimy Ridge, Mons, Kitchener. Hahndorf became Ambleside. Lobethal was nearly Marananga, until the locals campaigned and got Tweedvale.

But Marananga did land somewhere. Gnadenfrei, a Barossa settlement, became Marananga in nineteen eighteen, and Marananga it still is. According to the State Library's place-names index, the word comes from the language of people at Overland Corner, far up the River Murray, and means "my hands". So a German name was swapped for an Aboriginal word carried in from someone else's Country.

In nineteen thirty-five, Hahndorf, Lobethal and Klemzig got their names back, just in time, as the State Library drily notes, for World War Two. Other names came back later. Some never did.

Here's one for the next glass. Can you belong to a place and still speak another language at home? South Australia gave one answer in nineteen seventeen and another in nineteen thirty-five. Which one is it giving now?

## Script: Stop 6 — The Insect That Must Stay Out

*About 440 words, about 3.4 minutes.*

[SFX — vineyard, under]

Hemera was the Greek goddess of daylight. The estate nearby took her name, it says, because when the sun rises on the southern Barossa, its vineyard is one of the first places to feel the warmth. One block there has been feeling it for a long time: Grenache, planted in nineteen twelve, the year Hermann Thumm was born, and still in production.

The valley has a scale for this. Under the Barossa Old Vine Charter, vines of thirty-five years are Old, seventy are Survivors, a hundred are Centenarians, and a hundred and twenty-five are Ancestors. Those are ages, mind, not a promise that older always tastes better. So why are there still vines this old here? Let's ask a vine-health scientist. Ours is a composite voice, but the facts are Vinehealth Australia's.

[SFX — vineyard, stop]

So what could kill a vine that's lasted over a century?

SCIENTIST: An insect about a millimetre long, yellow to brown. Grape phylloxera. Not a fungus, not a virus. A bug, and it eats the roots.

How bad is that?

SCIENTIST: Once a vineyard's infested, the vines die within six years. There's no treatment that gets rid of it. You pull them out and replant on rootstock that can tolerate it, roots from North American grape species that evolved alongside the thing. Over ninety per cent of the world's vines are grafted onto fewer than ten rootstocks.

And here?

SCIENTIST: Here, about three quarters of South Australia's vineyards are on their own roots. That only works because phylloxera has never got in. It's in parts of Victoria and New South Wales, not here.

Luck?

SCIENTIST: Law. In eighteen seventy-four, with phylloxera doing incalculable damage to vineyards in Europe and America, South Australia banned vine material from infested places. In eighteen ninety-nine it set up a phylloxera board, and that board is still going. It's called Vinehealth Australia now.

So what's the risk now?

SCIENTIST: Anything that moves between vineyards. Machinery, equipment, grapes, cuttings, soil. That's why there are footwear protocols, and why visitors are asked to admire the vines from the paths.

[SFX — dusk, under]

After the tasting rooms close, there's another workforce. In twenty nineteen, eighteen Barossa growers joined a program called Wildlife for Wine, which welcomes microbats, birds of prey and insect hunters such as parasitic wasps, lacewings and spiders. A microbat can eat half its body weight in insects in a single night. What none of them can do is touch phylloxera, down on the roots. The night shift helps. The border is the defence.

[SFX — dusk, stop]

So, for the glass: a whole region's oldest vines depend on strangers keeping their boots clean. How much inconvenience would you accept to protect something irreplaceable that you'll never own?

## Script: Stop 7 — The Town That Held a Song Contest

*About 430 words, about 3.3 minutes, with the song segment.*

[SFX — hall, under]

In eighteen fifty-nine the Gawler Institute, in a town near today's route, celebrated its second birthday in style. It offered ten guineas for the best words for a national song, and ten guineas for the best music. Ninety-six people sent in words. Nobody recorded how many sent music.

The winners were Caroline Carleton, of English stock, for the words, and Carl Linger, German-born, for the music. The History Trust notes that between them they represented both sexes and the colony's two main settler groups, which, after today's story about German names, has a certain ring to it.

[SFX — hall, stop]

Their Song of Australia became the accepted national song in South Australia and, for a time, across the country. When Queen Elizabeth the Second landed at Circular Quay in nineteen fifty-four, it was played after God Save the Queen.

Then Australia got a vote. On the twenty-first of May, nineteen seventy-seven, a voluntary national poll offered four songs: Advance Australia Fair, Waltzing Matilda, God Save the Queen, and Gawler's own.

[SFX — ballot]

South Australia stayed loyal. Song of Australia led here, with about a third of first preferences. Nationally it came last, with under one vote in ten. Advance Australia Fair took about forty-four per cent, Waltzing Matilda about twenty-eight, God Save the Queen about nineteen. Waltzing Matilda won the Australian Capital Territory, Advance Australia Fair won everywhere else, and after preferences it became the national song. In an earlier poll, in nineteen seventy-four, it had taken nearly half of South Australia's vote. The tide was already going out.

It hasn't gone entirely. The History Trust says it's now remembered mainly by older South Australians, and still turns up now and then at functions in the Adelaide Town Hall. And the Adelaide Liedertafel, a choir Carl Linger founded, still sings it in his honour.

And remember this morning's two maps. A settler song from eighteen fifty-nine is one view of this country, not everyone's. So here's one for the ride home: if a national song were written this year, what would it have to include, and who would you trust to judge the entries?

The recording on the playlist is Peter Dawson's. He was born in Adelaide in eighteen eighty-two, a bass-baritone who began recording in nineteen oh four, and the State Library of South Australia holds more than four hundred of his recordings, more than of any other single artist. The sound is old-fashioned, so hear it as an artefact: the song a town chose, sung by a son of Adelaide. If you'd like to hear Song Of Australia, tap the song button on the screen.

## Script: Stop 8 — The Airport That Has No Runways

*About 370 words, about 2.8 minutes.*

[SFX — shorebirds, under]

You've had a long day of travel. Some of your neighbours on this coast have had a much longer year.

Along more than sixty kilometres of coast north of Adelaide runs the Adelaide International Bird Sanctuary. Its Kaurna name, Winaityinaityi Pangkara, means a country for all birds, and the country that surrounds these birds.

It sits at the southern end of the East Asian–Australasian Flyway, a migration route used by more than five million birds a year. Birds reach this coast from as far as Siberia and Alaska, passing through as many as twenty-two countries on the way. Between September and March, up to twenty-seven thousand shorebirds gather along it, and more than fifty species of migratory birds have been recorded here.

Take one traveller, the sharp-tailed sandpiper: about twenty-one centimetres long and around sixty grams. It breeds in the short Siberian summer, June to August, on the tundra of the high Arctic. Then it heads south, reaching Australia from August, and goes back to Siberia in March. Every year.

[SFX — shorebirds, stop]

Which brings us to the least glamorous habitat on the coast. BirdLife Australia's species notes say sharp-tailed sandpipers turn up around sewage farms. And near here at Bolivar, SA Water's treated-water polishing lagoons cover about five hundred hectares, and they're private. In twenty nineteen, SA Water and Birds SA reported sharp-tailed sandpipers there, among other shorebirds, ducks and fish-eating birds.

[SFX — wings]

So the airport with no runways runs partly on a water-treatment plant. Not every bird out there is a migrant, and that report is a snapshot from twenty nineteen, not a promise for this evening. But a bird flying half the planet needs safe places to land, feed and rest, and some of them turn out to be things people built for a completely different reason.

Which is not a bad summary of the whole day. A dolphin that picked up a trick from a tank. A winemaker who studied his way through an internment. A cellar that runs on promises. And a sewage works that doubles as an international terminal.

Last one for the evening: if you could follow one of these birds home to the Arctic, which leg of the journey would you most want to see?
