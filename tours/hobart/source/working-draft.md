# Hobart to Russell Falls

Eight short audio stories for the drive from Hobart to Mount Field and back. Narration from the research notes (local only, gitignored), which hold the sources and fact checks.

## Script: Stop 1 — The Road That Tried to Sink

Imagine being given this job.

Build a road across a river.

You have no bulldozer. No enormous truck. No crane waiting to lower a bridge into place.

You have a pick, a shovel, and a wheelbarrow.

And the bottom of the river is mud.

Near Granton, that was a real problem. In eighteen thirty, work began on the Bridgewater causeway: a bank of material pushed out across the shallow part of the Derwent.

But the river did not politely hold the road up.

Heavy stone pressed into soft ground. Foundations sank. More material had to go in.

Think about trying to make a path across a bowl of porridge. A pebble disappears. Another pebble disappears. How many will you need before there is something firm enough to stand on?

Of course, a river is much more complicated than porridge. Water moves. Mud shifts. A road has to carry weight.

The people doing this exhausting work were convicts: prisoners sent to the colony. As many as two hundred and eighty worked on the causeway at one time. Over the project, they moved roughly one point eight million tonnes of stone, soil and clay.

Those numbers describe an engineering achievement. They also describe years of hard labour imposed on people who could not simply choose another job.

There was an ingenious answer to some of the sinking. Fresh willow branches were used as a base beneath the embankment.

By eighteen thirty-six, the causeway was complete.

Almost.

A deep channel still separated its end from the opposite bank. So travellers used a small ferry for the final gap. Later, bridges completed the crossing.

A road across most of a river, followed by a tiny boat journey: that was once the practical solution.

The modern bridge opened in June twenty twenty-five. It belongs to a different world of machinery and construction, but it answers the same old question.

How do we get safely to the other side?

For the passengers, here is a challenge. Imagine you are designing a crossing. Would you build a high bridge, dig a tunnel, or use a ferry?

Each answer has something it does well.

And each has a problem waiting underneath.

## Notes: Stop 1

Direction: OUTBOUND. Trigger: Approaching Granton and the Bridgewater Bridge junction. Play once the route is clear; the passenger starts playback. The main Mount Field route stays on the Granton/New Norfolk side of the river: do not cross the bridge just for this story.

## Script: Stop 2 — The Town That Carried an Island in Its Name

Suppose someone told your family that you had to move.

Not to another room. Not to another house down the road.

Across the sea.

You could take some possessions. But the place you knew, the paths you walked, and the garden you had worked on would have to stay behind.

That is the beginning of the story hidden in the name New Norfolk.

Its older namesake is Norfolk Island, far out in the Pacific.

Britain established a colonial settlement there in seventeen eighty-eight. Among the people sent there were soldiers and convicts. Over time, people married, raised children, and built farms. Many former prisoners finished their sentences and became free.

For them, the island was home.

But the government wanted to close the settlement.

Many inhabitants did not want to leave. Between eighteen oh seven and eighteen oh eight, hundreds were moved to Hobart Town. Some received land in the district that became New Norfolk.

They had crossed the sea, but their difficulties were not over.

Food and clothing were scarce. Making a farm with poor tools was hard work. A promise of a new beginning did not make that beginning easy.

The name New Norfolk carried a connection with the island they had left.

It is like putting a memory on a map.

There is another part of this story to remember. The land where newcomers made their farms was Aboriginal Country. For Aboriginal people, the expansion of the colony brought dispossession. A place could be a new home to one family and part of a much older homeland to another.

Both belong in the history.

Norfolk Island has another famous story involving descendants of the Bounty mutineers. But that community arrived on Norfolk Island in eighteen fifty-six, decades after the migration that helped shape New Norfolk. The two journeys should not be mixed together.

For the passengers, imagine one small box that could keep a memory of somewhere you have loved.

Would you put in a photograph? A drawing? A shell you were allowed to collect? Perhaps a recipe, or the name of a friend?

What would you choose?

A town cannot fit inside a box.

But sometimes it carries a memory just as carefully.

Here, that memory is written in two words.

New Norfolk.

## Notes: Stop 2

Direction: OUTBOUND. Trigger: Entering New Norfolk on the way from Hobart, or after passing through the town if navigation needs quiet. No stop or town-centre detour is required.

## Script: Stop 3 — The Farm That Climbs into the Sky

Imagine arriving at a farm where the crop needs a ladder.

Not a little ladder leaning against a tree.

A whole field of climbing frames.

Around Bushy Park, tall poles and lines support a plant that spends the growing season reaching upwards. Its name is hops.

At first, the framework can look like an enormous piece of unfinished construction. But the building material is alive.

Hop stems climb by winding themselves around a support. Growers call these stems bines. Tiny hooked hairs help them hold on.

Give a growing shoot a string, and it can turn that string into a route towards the sunlight.

Why go to so much trouble?

Because the female plants produce little green cones used in brewing beer. Inside those cones are glands containing substances that contribute bitterness and aroma.

Think of a kitchen ingredient that is used in a small amount but makes a big difference. A squeeze of lemon can change a sauce. A pinch of spice can change a meal.

Hops can change the character of a drink.

The history here reaches back through generations of the Shoobridge family. William Shoobridge established a successful hop plantation in Tasmania in eighteen twenty-two. His son Ebenezer later developed the enterprise at Bushy Park.

By the eighteen forties, the Derwent Valley had already become Tasmania's leading hop-growing region.

For many years, harvest brought work for men, women and families. Cones had to be gathered, then dried. Special drying buildings called oast houses became part of the landscape.

An oast house is a clue that farming does not finish when something is picked. Sometimes the next stage is just as important as growing the crop.

We are travelling in spring, well before the main local harvest, which begins around March. The fields may show a different stage of growth from the tall green walls seen in harvest photographs.

That is part of the interest. A farm is always changing.

For the passengers, invent a plant that would need an unusual farm.

A pumpkin that grows on a tower? Strawberries on a spiral staircase? A bean that needs its own bridge?

Then ask the farmer's question.

How would you harvest it?

These real fields are one answer: build the support, guide the climbing stems, and let the growing season do its work.

## Notes: Stop 3

Direction: OUTBOUND. Trigger: Around Bushy Park/Glenora, when passing the hop-growing district. Use the place name as the trigger, not a promise that a particular field is visible. The main road is sufficient; farm entry is not part of the tour.

## Script: Stop 4 — The Train, the Ranger and the Horse

Before this became a car trip, it could be a railway adventure.

Imagine a family setting off from Hobart more than a hundred years ago. Picture their picnic packed, their walking shoes ready, and the exciting possibility of a waterfall waiting at the other end.

That family is imagined. The railway journey was real.

The Derwent Valley line reached Westerway in nineteen oh nine. In nineteen seventeen, an extension brought the railway to National Park, near the entrance to what we call Mount Field.

People could travel into this district to walk, fish, and visit the falls. A guesthouse had already been built near the present park entrance in nineteen eleven.

But a railway and a guesthouse could not do everything.

Visitors still needed paths.

And paths through mountain country do not appear simply because somebody draws a line on a map.

One of the people who helped make this park accessible was its first ranger, Bill Belcher, appointed in nineteen seventeen.

He had a helper with four legs.

A horse named Runic.

Belcher cut tracks, and Runic helped haul materials for tracks and huts. Think about that partnership: a person deciding where work was needed, and a strong animal carrying things that would be difficult to move by hand.

Now imagine being responsible for a path through wet forest.

Where should it go? How do you cross a stream? What happens when a tree falls? Where can walkers rest without damaging the place they have come to enjoy?

A good path can make an enormous landscape feel welcoming.

But this was never a place without human history. Mount Field is part of the homelands of the Big River Nation of Tasmanian Aboriginal people. Their connection to Country reaches far beyond the arrival of trains or tourists.

The national park was declared in nineteen sixteen, alongside Freycinet, as one of Tasmania's first two national parks.

The following year, a train could help bring visitors here.

And a ranger and his horse helped them continue on foot.

When we walk, we can notice how much thought goes into the ordinary things beneath our shoes: a step, a bridge, a place where the path bends around a tree.

Someone had to make those choices.

For the passengers, one final question.

If you were designing a forest path, what is the very first thing you would check?

## Notes: Stop 4

Direction: OUTBOUND. Trigger: Leaving Westerway towards National Park/Mount Field. If there is insufficient time before arrival, finish this while parked. An optional landmark is the old Westerway railway station; do not rely on the station being open.

## Script: Stop 5 — The Giants with Flowers

A giant is waiting in the forest.

It has no feet, yet it lifts itself high into the sky.

It drinks without a mouth.

And somewhere far above our heads, it makes flowers.

The giant is a swamp gum, also called mountain ash. Its scientific name is Eucalyptus regnans.

That last word is connected with ruling, or reigning.

A good name for a tree that towers over its neighbours.

This species can grow more than a hundred metres tall. The trees beside our own walk are not all that height, but Parks identifies several along the Russell Falls track that exceed seventy metres.

Before we reach one, try to picture that distance standing upright.

Then imagine trying to build such a tower from a seed.

There is no crane lifting the top into position. The tree grows from living tissue, making wood and leaves as the years pass.

Its leaves use sunlight to help make sugars from water and carbon dioxide. Some of the material a tree builds comes, remarkably, from the air.

A little plant becomes a sapling.

A sapling becomes a tree.

Given the right conditions and enough time, a tree becomes a landmark.

Some of the great swamp gums here have been growing for hundreds of years.

But this forest has more than one level.

Beneath the tallest trees are smaller trees and tree ferns. Near the ground are mosses, fallen leaves, and the remains of old trunks.

A fallen tree can look like the end of a story.

Look more closely.

As wood breaks down, it becomes part of the forest's continuing life. A trunk that once reached towards the sky can become shelter, a damp surface for small plants, and a store of material gradually returned to the ground.

Standing giants and fallen giants belong to the same forest.

This is also Country with a long human history. Tasmanian Aboriginal people knew these landscapes long before national park boundaries were drawn. We are visitors to a living place, rather than explorers arriving where nobody has ever been.

When we step out, the passengers have three quiet challenges.

Find something enormous.

Find something so small that an adult might walk straight past it.

And find something that looks dead but is helping something else live.

The giant tree might be the easiest of the three.

## Notes: Stop 5

## Script: Stop 6 — The Hunter with an Electric Face

Somewhere in a Tasmanian river, a hunter dives.

It closes its eyes.

It closes its ears.

It closes its nostrils.

Then it starts looking for dinner.

That sounds like a terrible plan.

Unless your face can detect electricity.

The hunter is a platypus.

Its bill contains receptors that detect tiny electrical signals produced by other living things. It can also sense movement and pressure.

Imagine trying to find a shrimp under dark water, where seeing is difficult. The platypus has a way of gathering information that a human face simply does not have.

This is real biology, although it sounds like equipment from a spy story.

The animal does not need to zap its dinner. Detecting electricity is different from making an electric shock.

It is more like receiving a secret message.

A platypus feeds on small water animals, including insect larvae, worms and crustaceans. Its webbed front feet help drive it through the water, while its broad tail has another useful job: storing fat.

And the surprises do not end there.

It has fur.

Its mother produces milk.

Yet she lays eggs.

Platypuses and echidnas belong to the group of egg-laying mammals called monotremes. Being a mammal does not always mean having babies in the way a dog or a person does.

When European naturalists first examined platypus specimens, some doubted that such an animal could be real.

That reaction tells us something about the scientists, too.

They were trying to understand a creature using the animals they already knew.

Nature had come up with a combination that did not fit their expectations.

The Tyenna River and nearby waterways are places where platypuses live. We might see one, or we might not. A wild animal has no appointment with our car.

If we later watch from a safe riverbank, the best tools are patience and quiet. We can look for a low shape on the surface and watch what it does, rather than deciding that every ripple must be a platypus.

For now, imagine designing your own river explorer.

Would you give it a lamp? A camera? Whiskers?

A platypus comes with a different answer.

Keep the eyes shut.

Let the bill do the detecting.

## Notes: Stop 6

Direction: RETURN. Trigger: Leaving Mount Field along the Tyenna River towards Westerway. Also works parked before a quiet river watch. Do not stop on the roadside just because this track mentions water.

## Script: Stop 7 — The Cargo That Must Not Hatch

There is a ship crossing the world.

Inside it are boxes.

Inside the boxes are thousands of living eggs.

And around the eggs is something very important.

Ice.

The year is eighteen sixty-four. The ship is the Norfolk. Its cargo includes salmon and trout eggs being carried from Britain towards Australia.

The people organising the voyage want the eggs to survive.

But they do not want them to hatch too soon.

Imagine a chick hatching halfway through a journey when nobody has brought anything for it to eat. For a newly hatched fish, an unsuitable travelling box would be a very poor nursery.

Keeping the eggs cold helped slow their development.

But how do you keep something cold on a long sea voyage before modern electric refrigeration?

You bring a great deal of ice.

The eggs were packed with damp moss inside an icehouse on the ship. Earlier attempts to bring these fish across the world had failed.

This time, the experiment worked.

The Norfolk reached Melbourne with living eggs. The precious cargo continued to Tasmania and then towards the hatchery at Plenty, where the Salmon Ponds had been prepared.

Think of the final stage.

After all that ocean, the future of the experiment depended on tiny eggs arriving safely at some carefully made ponds beside a little Tasmanian river.

In May, the first young fish began to emerge.

But there was a twist.

Salmon had been the great ambition. People hoped the fish would go to sea and return to the river.

That did not produce the hoped-for self-sustaining salmon population.

The brown trout were the lasting success. They established themselves, and eggs from these ponds later helped stock other hatcheries in Australia and New Zealand.

Something included alongside the main plan became the most enduring result.

There is another side to that success. These were introduced fish. Tasmania's rivers already had native animals, and trout can harm native fish populations.

So this is both an extraordinary transport story and a reminder that moving a species can change a place.

If we feed fish at the ponds, imagine the fragile cargo behind the history.

Not a tank full of splashing fish.

Not even one little fish swimming.

Just an egg.

Kept alive across an ocean by planning, persistence, and a shipload of melting ice.

## Notes: Stop 7

Direction: RETURN / BEFORE SALMON PONDS. Trigger: On the return drive approaching Plenty, preferably 5-10 minutes before reaching Salmon Ponds. If you skip Salmon Ponds, play on the same stretch: the story still works without visiting.

## Script: Stop 8 — The Forest That Became the News

Suppose somebody handed you a piece of wood.

Then asked you to turn it into tomorrow's news.

You could carve a headline into it.

But that would be a very heavy newspaper.

The more useful answer is hidden inside the wood.

Fibres.

Paper is made from a thin sheet of fibres joined together. To get them from a tree into a page, you have to separate them, mix them with water, spread them out, and remove most of that water again.

That is the idea.

Doing it reliably, in enormous quantities, is an engineering challenge.

Near New Norfolk, at Boyer, people took on a particularly difficult version.

Could Australian eucalyptus hardwood be used to make newsprint?

Many experts had considered it unsuitable. Its fibres are shorter than those of the softwoods commonly used for paper.

Researchers and mill workers had to find a process that would make the material useful.

On the twenty-second of February, nineteen forty-one, the Boyer mill produced Australia's first newsprint.

Its original recipe was not all eucalyptus. It combined local hardwood pulp with imported softwood pulp.

Even so, making newsprint mainly from eucalyptus hardwood was a remarkable achievement.

Now remember the date.

Nineteen forty-one.

The Second World War was underway. Australia depended on sea routes for many supplies, and shipping was under pressure.

Newsprint made in Tasmania helped Australian newspapers keep printing. Ten daily newspapers used Boyer paper when wartime shortages threatened supplies.

Imagine the journey of one piece.

A tree in Tasmania.

Fibres separated in a mill.

A wide roll of paper.

A printing press.

Then a newspaper landing in someone's hands, carrying reports from a world at war.

The same forest country that drew people here for waterfalls and walking also supplied industry.

And that created arguments. Demand for timber helped drive a dispute over removing land from Mount Field National Park.

Jobs, useful products and the protection of forests became connected questions.

For the passengers, here is a small invention challenge.

Choose something ordinary in the car: a book, a cardboard box, a seat belt, or a window.

What raw material did it begin as?

What had to happen before it became useful?

A finished object can hide a long adventure.

At Boyer, one of those adventures began with wood and ended with the world reading the news.

## Notes: Stop 8

Direction: RETURN. Trigger: After returning through New Norfolk, heading towards Hobart along the Derwent. Boyer Mill stands across the river from this part of the route; do not divert onto Boyer Road or cross the river to play this. Visibility of the mill is not required.
